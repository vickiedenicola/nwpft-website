The Issue

Wild pigs are one of North America’s most destructive invasive species

Wild pigs (also called feral swine, feral hogs, or wild boar) are a non-native, invasive species of great biological, ecological, cultural, and economic concern. They reproduce quickly, adapt to nearly any habitat, and damage almost everything they encounter. Their range continues to expand, and no single landowner, agency, or state can manage the problem alone.

That is the reason the National Wild Pig Task Force exists: to coordinate a science-based response across the jurisdictions and disciplines the problem spans.

Why they spread

Built to multiply and move

A handful of traits make wild pigs uniquely difficult to control compared with most invasive wildlife.

Rapid reproduction

Sows can breed before their first year and produce multiple litters, so populations rebound quickly unless removal is sustained and complete.

Highly adaptable

Wild pigs thrive in forests, wetlands, rangeland, and farmland alike, shifting diet and behavior to whatever a landscape offers.

No respect for boundaries

Sounders move freely across property and state lines, which means fragmented, uncoordinated efforts rarely keep pace.

The impacts

Damage that reaches well beyond the farm

Wild pig damage is measured in billions of dollars each year, but the costs extend into ecosystems, water, wildlife, and human and animal health.

Agriculture & forestry

Rooting and wallowing destroy crops, pastures, food plots, and replanted timber, and damage equipment and fences.

Natural resources

Their rooting erodes soil, degrades water quality, and disrupts sensitive habitats and native plant communities.

Native wildlife

Wild pigs prey on ground-nesting birds, reptiles, and amphibians and compete with native species for food.

Disease

They can carry and transmit diseases and parasites that threaten livestock, pets, wildlife, and people.

Property & infrastructure

Damage to levees, roadsides, lawns, and golf courses, plus vehicle collisions, adds to the toll.

Expanding range

Once largely a Southern problem, established populations now reach across much of the country and continue to grow.

A problem no one can solve alone

Because wild pigs cross every kind of boundary, effective management depends on researchers, agencies, and land managers working from a shared understanding and a coordinated plan.

That coordination (connecting the science, the practitioners, and the policy on one continent-wide front) is the work of the Task Force.
